Gentle Kitchen · product brief

Who we're cooking for, and who's in the kitchen

Goal: an app you rely on every week for Crohn's with mild strictures, built so it can later open up to other people with IBD. These personas are fictional composites. Each one gives us a lens to check new recipes and features against.

People we cook for

You come first. The other five show where the app needs to stretch when it opens up to others.

You

The founder

Vegetarian (eggs and dairy), mild strictures, mushrooms are a known trigger. Primary user.

"I want to open the app and find something I can safely eat, and actually want to eat, in under a minute."
Needs
  • Recipes already checked against strictures and triggers
  • Real photos so a dish looks worth cooking
  • Batch and freezer meals for low-energy days
Frustrations
  • Generic IBD advice that ignores vegetarians
  • Recipe sites full of seeds, skins and chilli
Would love
  • A weekly plan with one shopping list
  • A way to learn which meals sit well
M

Maya, 22

Student, diagnosed four months ago. Small budget, shared kitchen, basic cooking skills.

"I'm scared of food right now. I need someone to tell me what's OK and why."
Needs
  • Cheap recipes with five ingredients or fewer
  • Plain explanations of why each one is gentle
  • Confidence to try new foods slowly
Frustrations
  • Conflicting advice online
  • Recipes that assume a blender or fancy kit
Would love
  • "Beginner" and "budget" filters
  • A simple "start here" guide
T

Tom, 44

Parent cooking for a 15-year-old with Crohn's, plus the rest of the family.

"I can't cook three dinners a night. I need one meal everyone will eat."
Needs
  • Family-sized portions and a servings scaler
  • Food a teenager actually likes
  • Packed lunches for school
Frustrations
  • "Special diet" food the rest of the family won't touch
  • Worry about growth and weight
Would love
  • Add-ons (e.g. chilli on the side) for others at the table
  • A shared shopping list
A

Aisha, 35

Nurse on rotating shifts, in remission with a stricture. Eats fish and chicken.

"Sunday is my cooking day. Everything after that comes out of the freezer."
Needs
  • Batch-cook plans with a timed order of work
  • Freezer tracking with use-by dates
  • Portable lunches for long shifts
Frustrations
  • Apps that are all-vegetarian or all-meat
  • Losing track of what's in the freezer
Would love
  • Diet profiles: veggie, pescatarian, omnivore
  • "Cook once, eat four times" plans
G

Graham, 61

Had part of his bowel removed last year. Lives alone, tires easily, not confident with apps.

"Big writing, few buttons, and nothing that takes more than half an hour on my feet."
Needs
  • Low-residue meals with soft textures
  • Large text and simple screens
  • Single portions that freeze well
Frustrations
  • Small text and fiddly menus
  • Long recipes with lots of standing
Would love
  • A cook mode that keeps the screen on
  • Sit-down-friendly recipes
L

Leah, 29

Vegan with Crohn's. Low iron, often tired, worried she isn't getting enough protein.

"Every IBD list tells me to eat chicken and white fish. That doesn't help me."
Needs
  • Plant protein that's gentle (tofu, smooth lentils, nut butters)
  • Iron, B12 and calcium flagged on recipes
Frustrations
  • Pulses and wholegrains being "banned" outright
  • Meat substitutes that are heavily processed
Would love
  • Vegan swaps on every recipe
  • Nutrient tags and a weekly protein check

The build team

Six specialist voices to run ideas past. When we plan a feature or a recipe batch, each one asks their question.

PM

Sam, product manager

Keeps the app focused on the jobs people actually need done.

"Does this help someone find a safe meal they want, faster?"
Pushes for
  • One measure of success: time to a meal you'll cook
  • Build for you now, design so others fit later
  • Small releases you test in your own kitchen
Watches out for
  • Feature creep before the basics are loved
  • Anything that reads like medical advice
RD

Helen, IBD dietitian

Registered dietitian specialising in inflammatory bowel disease.

"With strictures, texture matters more than fibre totals. And no diet should get narrower than it needs to."
Pushes for
  • Texture levels on every recipe: smooth, soft, regular
  • Protein, iron, B12, calcium and vitamin D for vegetarians
  • Portion and "start small" guidance when trying foods
  • A food and symptom diary to find personal triggers
Watches out for
  • Over-restriction leading to weight loss or deficiencies
  • Treating all processed food as bad; supplement drinks and tinned staples can help
CH

Marco, chef

Recipe developer. Makes gentle food taste of something.

"Mild doesn't mean bland. Herbs, browning, citrus and good stock do the work chilli usually does."
Pushes for
  • Base recipes: one sauce, stock or purée that feeds five meals
  • Technique tips: peeling, blending, slow-cooking until soft
  • Swaps on each recipe (dairy-free, vegan, cheaper)
  • Every recipe cooked and photographed by us
Watches out for
  • Beige-food fatigue; the menu needs colour and variety
  • Untested quantities and timings
UX

Jo, UX and accessibility

Designs for fatigue, brain fog and one-handed use in a kitchen.

"On a bad day, every extra tap is a reason to give up and eat toast."
Pushes for
  • Cook mode: big steps, screen stays on, built-in timers
  • Larger-text option and strong contrast
  • Kind, guilt-free wording (no "bad foods")
Watches out for
  • Cluttered screens as features pile up
  • Tracking that feels like homework
TL

Dev, tech lead

Keeps it simple, fast, offline-friendly and cheap to run.

"Your data lives on your phone until there's a good reason, and your permission, to move it."
Pushes for
  • Your own photos, stored on the phone
  • Recipe data that supports diet profiles and texture levels
  • Auto-deploy to Netlify on every GitHub change
  • Backup and export so nothing is lost
Watches out for
  • Accounts and servers before they're needed
  • Storing health data without a plan
PR

Nadia, privacy and regulation

Advises on UK GDPR and health-app rules.

"A recipe app is fine. An app that tells someone how to treat their disease is a medical device."
Pushes for
  • Clear "not medical advice" wording, with signposting to IBD teams
  • Treat symptom data as sensitive health data
  • A data protection impact assessment (DPIA) before other users join
  • A recorded review process for recipe content
Watches out for
  • Personalised medical recommendations
  • Analytics or third parties seeing health data

Where they agree

Five ideas came up from several personas at once. These should shape the next round of work.

Texture is the stricture question

Label every recipe smooth, soft or regular, and let people filter by it. This grows naturally out of today's flare-day switch.

Helen · Graham · you

Energy is the scarcest ingredient

Batch plans, freezer tracking, five-ingredient meals and a cook mode matter more than adding hundreds of recipes.

Aisha · Graham · Jo · Marco

Vegetarians need a nutrition safety net

Show protein and iron per portion, and flag B12, calcium and vitamin D sources, so a gentle diet doesn't become a thin one.

Helen · Leah · you

Triggers are personal

The avoid list is a start. A light "how did that sit?" note after meals would show patterns over time.

Helen · Maya · you

Trust has to be earned

Real photos, tested recipes, a dietitian review and plain "why it's gentle" notes. Never promise to treat anything.

Marco · Nadia · Maya

Suggested roadmap

Built for you first. Each item names the persona who'd champion it. Nothing is locked in, so reorder freely.

Now

For you, next few sessions
  1. Your own photosSnap a dish and attach it to the recipe. (Dev, Marco)
  2. Texture levels and filterSmooth, soft, regular on all recipes. (Helen)
  3. Cook modeLarge steps, screen stays awake, timers. (Jo, Graham)
  4. Servings scalerCook for one or a batch of six. (Tom, Aisha)
  5. 20+ new recipesHigh-protein, iron-rich, five-ingredient and freezer meals. (Marco, Helen)

Next

Make it a weekly habit
  1. Week plannerPick meals, get one shopping list grouped by aisle. (Sam, Aisha)
  2. Freezer trackerUses the storage times already on each recipe. (Aisha)
  3. "How did it sit?" notesPrivate, optional, kept on your phone. (Helen, Nadia)
  4. Nutrition tagsProtein, iron and calcium per portion. (Helen, Leah)
  5. Swaps and auto-deployRecipe swaps; Netlify updates on every GitHub change. (Marco, Dev)

Later

Opening up to others
  1. Diet profilesVegetarian, vegan, pescatarian, omnivore. (Aisha, Leah)
  2. Dietitian reviewPaid review of the whole recipe set, with a badge. (Helen, Nadia)
  3. Accessibility settingsLarger text, simpler layout. (Jo, Graham)
  4. Accounts and syncOnly after a DPIA and clear consent. (Dev, Nadia)
  5. Sharing and clinic exportShare recipes; export notes for appointments. (Tom, Helen)

Questions for you

Your answers decide the order of the roadmap.

Which "Now" item would make the biggest difference to your week?

Photos, texture levels, cook mode, servings scaler or more recipes.

How do you usually cook?

A big weekend batch, a little every day, or a mix? This shapes the planner and freezer tools.

What kitchen kit do you have?

Blender, stick blender, slow cooker, pressure cooker, air fryer, freezer space.

Would a "how did it sit?" note help, or feel like too much tracking?

It's the dietitian's favourite idea, but only if it's worth your energy.

Beyond mushrooms, which foods do you already know suit you, or don't?

These become your defaults and guide the next batch of recipes.

When you open it up, who comes first?

Friends with IBD, a support group, or the public? This decides when accounts, privacy work and a dietitian review are needed.

The personas are fictional composites written to guide design. They are not real people and not clinical guidance. Diet changes for Crohn's should be checked with an IBD team or registered dietitian.
